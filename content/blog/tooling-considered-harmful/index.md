Developers love tools. We use them to remove friction, automate repetition, and extend what we can build. Yet tools can also occupy so much mental space that we stop making deliberate choices and begin following whatever workflow they impose.

The smartphone offers an obvious example. It was meant to keep us mobile and connected, but for many people it has become a source of distraction and dependence. Development tools can create a milder version of the same problem. We try every new editor, plugin, and service while mastering none of them.

I used to chase each promising tool. Now I judge one against three questions:

- **Stability:** Does it have a dependable core and ecosystem?
- **Performance:** Does it respect my CPU and memory?
- **Responsiveness:** Can it keep pace with my thoughts without interrupting the work?

For me, Vim answers all three.

My configuration is fast, functional, and familiar enough to feel like an extension of my hands. Strong muscle memory means I no longer pause to recall how to navigate, edit, or refactor text. A small set of plugins—YouCompleteMe, ALE, and Fugitive—covers the essentials without turning the editor into a system I must constantly maintain.

I also stopped keeping a fallback editor. Committing to Vim forced me through the awkward moments and taught me shortcuts I would otherwise have avoided. Watching experienced Vim users such as Armin Ronacher and Gary Bernhardt work has helped too; seeing a tool used fluently often teaches more than reading another feature list.

This is not an argument against IDEs. The specific tool matters less than the depth of your command over it. If an IDE suits your work, learn it thoroughly. Build the muscle memory, understand its debugging and navigation features, and shape it around a stable workflow.

The goal is not minimalism for its own sake. It is to reduce the distance between thought and execution. Time and attention are limited; our tools should preserve both.
